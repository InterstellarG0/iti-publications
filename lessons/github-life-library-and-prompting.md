# ITI Field Manual: The Subfolders of Life

November 2026 • Founder-directed educational draft • Learning outcomes unevaluated

## Everyday lesson: The Subfolders of Life

A GitHub project library, no coding required.

GitHub can hold learning notes, project plans and records of changes. You can create and edit text in its web interface without writing software. A repository is a collection of files with version history. A README introduces the collection. A commit records a saved change with a short explanation. History records what was saved, not whether a claim is true.

Learning goal: create a small, readable project record, revise it and explain what changed. Learners can do the entire exercise with paper or local files. GitHub requires users to be at least 13, with higher local minimums where applicable. Younger learners use the offline exercise or watch an adult demonstration without operating a GitHub account.

### Browser steps

1. Choose a harmless fictional project, such as learning to draw. Decide what belongs in the record before uploading anything.
2. Eligible users sign in to their own account. Select **New repository**, choose a name such as `drawing-notebook`, select **Private**, enable **Add README**, and create it.
3. Open `README.md` and select the edit control. Explain the project's purpose, which files belong here and the next action. Preview the text and commit the change with a meaningful message, such as `Describe drawing practice goal`.
4. Use **Add file** and **Create new file** for a note, or **Upload files** for an existing small file. Plain text is enough. Start with `notes.md`, `projects.md` and `decisions.md`.
5. Record one fictional practice session. Separate what happened from an idea about what to try next. Commit with a message that explains the change.
6. Open the file's history and inspect an earlier change. Explain what changed and why. The record can contain a mistake even when the save succeeded.
7. Reconcile weekly: compare the plan, files and actual completed work. Mark completed, pending, blocked, deferred or uncertain. Link evidence where available. Correct stale claims and preserve the reason for corrections.
8. Keep a separate copy you can open. A downloaded ZIP is a snapshot of files, not the full Git history. Choose a proper repository backup workflow if preserving history is required.

Keep passwords, access tokens, addresses, medical records and private conversations out of the practice repository. Private visibility limits access but does not make a repository a confidential vault. Deleting today's file does not necessarily remove earlier versions or copies. Make a separate reviewed public derivative when sharing useful lessons. Permission to organize notes does not automatically authorize publication or collaborator access.

### See one, do one, teach one

See one: the facilitator creates an invented drawing-project note and saves a correction.

Do one: the learner writes a project goal, one observation and a next step. Save a second version and say what changed.

Teach one: explain a repository, README and commit in everyday words. Demonstrate how to tell a plan from completed work. Participation and personal disclosure are optional.

Success check: the learner can locate a note, explain its change history, identify what remains uncertain and name one kind of information to keep out. A GitHub account and an AI account are unnecessary for this check.

## Everyday lesson: Better Input. Better Output.

**Say what you need. Check what you get.**

Useful input states the goal, relevant context, constraints and desired format. More words alone do not guarantee better results. A clear request can still produce a wrong answer.

Example: `Plan a 20-minute drawing practice. I have paper and a pencil. Give four short steps and one way to check progress. Do not assume I own other supplies.`

Make requirements checkable. For the fictional inventory example, the response must use a target of 10 iron and an inventory of 7, and show 3 still needed. A model's output may vary. Deterministic checks and well-designed constraints can reject particular mistakes without making the model universally reliable.

Use a paper exercise too: a partner follows the request using only the supplied information. Compare the answer against the request, revise one missing detail and try again.

**Inspiration credit:** Lauren Tan ([@poteto](https://github.com/poteto)) and her AI Engineer CODE talk, [All Code Is Slop (Except Mine)](https://ai.engineer/code/2026/talks/lauren-tan). The founder connected the talk's discussion of input quality, engineering constraints and verification to everyday learning. The title and prompts here are ITI's educational adaptation, not a quotation or endorsement from Tan or her team.

Her comments policy concerned preventing agents from preserving bad workarounds in her team's product. This lesson does not turn that into a universal ban on documentation. Clear examples, structure and checks matter alongside prompt wording.

## Copyable continuity handoff

Use only material the learner is allowed to share. Review inputs before pasting them into any service. A prompt requests behavior; it does not establish privacy guarantees or prove that actions occurred.

```text
Help me maintain a project library without coding.

Goal: [the project or learning goal]
Audience: [me / a class / a reviewed public audience]
Starting point: [last confirmed version or file]
Allowed source material: [safe notes pasted below]
Keep private: [categories to exclude, without pasting their contents]
Decisions already made: [decisions and constraints]
Desired result: [a short note / file plan / checklist / handoff]

Preserve my original wording separately from your interpretation.
Identify who contributed each idea when known. Do not invent attribution.
Separate observations, hypotheses, decisions and verified results.
Preserve useful disagreements and corrections with their reasons.
Compare the new notes against the last confirmed record.
Flag conflicts or missing evidence. Ask only questions needed to proceed.
Keep meaningful filenames and existing lesson or topic identifiers.
Give a file-by-file change summary and the next unresolved step.
Do not call something saved, tested, scheduled or published without evidence.
Do not publish, delete files, invite people or change permissions from this
handoff alone. State what access or explicit authorization is needed.

Return:
1. Goal and current baseline
2. Completed work and evidence
3. Pending, blocked, deferred and uncertain items
4. Corrections and disagreements
5. Next action and who owns it
6. Source references and what you could not verify

Safe notes: [insert fictional or reviewed nonsensitive material]
```

## Repeatable lesson format

Each lesson includes a situation, a skill, a fictional example, a copyable practice prompt, a way to check the answer and a next action. Include an offline alternative. Model the process, let the learner adapt it, then invite them to explain it to someone else. Do not require a child to disclose personal information or use a service below its minimum age.

Small slide footer:

*AI can be wrong. Check important answers. Keep personal information private.*

Prompts accompany the skill instead of replacing the learner's judgment. Evaluate transfer with a new fictional situation, not likes, shares or fluent AI output.

## Leadership, recognition and contribution

Founder perspective: the founder commends Elon Musk for the room he believes Musk gives teams to build and for using his platform to support their products. This is the founder's interpretation of leadership, not a verified account of every workplace or a claim about how individual engineers feel.

The transferable practice is to make room for others to create, credit specific contributors and help useful work reach people. Preserve credit to Lauren Tan and her team rather than assigning all of their engineering work to a leader.

The founder proposes recognition, trust and visibility as nonfinancial incentives that may encourage continued contribution to open models or other open-source work. Their effect is a hypothesis here, not a measured outcome. Appreciation can accompany fair compensation. Public promotion does not establish that a product is open source; review its actual license and access terms. No claim is made here that Grok Bot is open source.
