# Take Initiative. Own the Outcome.
Date: 2026-11-27
Status: Seven-slide script draft; field-manual teaching note; unevaluated.
Origin: Founder-directed discussion of initiative, operational reserves and learning from exceptions; AI-assisted public adaptation. Examples below are fictional. Personal accounts and internal budgets are excluded.

## Seven-slide script
### Slide 1 — Take Initiative. Own the Outcome.
Sometimes a plan meets a problem nobody predicted.
Use your judgment. Explain your decision. Learn from the result.

### Slide 2 — Know the objective
What are you trying to accomplish?
Who depends on the work?
Food, rest and workable travel arrangements belong in planning for the people delivering it.

### Slide 3 — Check your room to act
What authority and resources do you have?
Can you reach the responsible person?
If you must decide, choose a proportionate option you can explain. Good intentions do not create permission.

### Slide 4 — Leave room in the plan
Reserve money, time and supplies for unexpected needs.
A club might budget for a missing cable so its event can continue.
Leave people room to rest, learn, earn and play, too.

### Slide 5 — Record the trade-off
Write what changed, which options were available, why you chose one, and what it cost.
Keep receipts or relevant evidence without exposing private information.
Report promptly; do not hide an exception.

### Slide 6 — Turn the exception into a lesson
Did the decision help? What needs repair?
Could clearer instructions or a better reserve prevent a repeat?
Workers own their decisions. Managers improve the conditions and guidance.

### Slide 7 — Practice the judgment
A club's display cable fails before an event. You have permission to use a small supply reserve.
Compare borrowing a cable, buying one within your limit, or changing the activity.
Explain your choice, record the result, and teach the next organizer.

## Field-manual application
Before work: define the objective, decision authority, spending limits, escalation route and reserve. Protect the people doing the work.
During an exception: assess necessity, alternatives, proportionality and consequences; use the agreed authority, document the decision and report it promptly. A reserve is not blanket spending permission. Urgent circumstances do not erase consent or professional safety requirements.
Afterward: review outcomes without treating a successful result as proof that every choice was sound. Make any repair, account for reserve use, and update budgets, instructions and training when patterns recur.
An apology should lead to repair and learning; predictable operational needs should become part of planning.

## Later paper development
When time and resources permit, expand the seven-slide argument into a sourced paper: distinguish personal experience from evidence; investigate reserve sizing, delegated authority, exception reporting and worker support; test whether clearer guidance improves decisions and service reliability. No measured improvement in sales, safety or learning is claimed.
