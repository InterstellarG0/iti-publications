# Privacy Without Constant Suspicion
**Author:** Anthony P. — Interstellar Technologies Initiative, LLC (ITI)  
**Date:** November 13, 2026  
**Status:** Field-manual lesson v0.1; unevaluated  
**Origin:** Public adaptation of the private ITI field manual's November 13 privacy lesson. AI-assisted organization; private autobiographical context excluded.

## Purpose
Teach a deliberate sharing decision without requiring the learner to assume that everybody is watching. A message can be forwarded; that possibility alone is not evidence that it was forwarded.

After the exercise, a learner should be able to identify unnecessary personal information, choose an audience, ask before sharing someone else's image, and distinguish a possibility from an observed event.

## Routine: pause, choose, share
1. Name the purpose: what does the intended recipient actually need?
2. Remove unnecessary passwords, home addresses, live locations and other people's private details from a public draft.
3. Choose an appropriate audience rather than defaulting to the broadest one.
4. Obtain permission before recording or publishing someone else's image.
5. If a problem occurs, describe the specific evidence and ask an appropriate trusted person for help.
6. Finish the check and return to the activity. Endless checking is not the objective.

For young learners, an adult can help review unfamiliar permission requests. Never ask learners to reveal real credentials or private messages to complete this exercise.

## Worked fictional example
A club drafts a public event invitation containing the organizer's home address, personal phone number and a participant photograph. The event itself will happen at a public venue.

The learner keeps the approved event location, date and purpose, replaces personal contact details with a designated club contact, and removes the photograph until permission is established. A venue address may be necessary to attend; the organizer's home address is not necessary for this invitation.

This example is invented. The appropriate contact and publication permissions must be defined for a real event.

## See one, do one, teach one
**See one:** an instructor explains one change and why it preserves the invitation's usefulness.  
**Do one:** the learner revises a second fictional invitation and marks any uncertain permission.  
**Teach one:** the learner explains the decision to a peer and distinguishes “someone could share this” from “I have evidence that someone shared this.”

## Evaluation card
| Check | Evidence to record |
|---|---|
| Necessary information retained | Can the reader understand and attend the fictional event? |
| Unnecessary details removed | Which details were removed, and why? |
| Consent uncertainty recognized | Did the learner ask before including another person's photo? |
| Possibility separated from evidence | Can the learner explain the distinction in their own words? |
| Help remains accessible | Can they identify an appropriate person to ask? |
| Burden proportionate | Can they finish without repeated checking? |

Record misunderstandings as well as correct answers. One completed exercise does not establish lasting behavior change.

## Limits
This is a proposed digital-literacy exercise, not a device-security assessment, legal guarantee, or clinical intervention. It does not prove anyone is being monitored. Deleting a post does not guarantee removal of copies already shared. Responsibility for misuse remains with the person misusing information; this lesson must not blame someone whose privacy was violated.

A useful next test is a small voluntary exercise using synthetic invitations, followed by revision of confusing instructions. No learner results are reported here.
