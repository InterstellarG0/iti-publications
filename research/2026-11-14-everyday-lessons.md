# Everyday Lessons: Boundaries, Privacy, and Attention

**Date:** November 2026  
**Status:** Educational design proposal v0.1; learning outcomes unevaluated  
**Origin:** Founder-directed lesson development with AI-assisted drafting. This public adaptation excludes personal conversations and internal operating records.

## Purpose
Create short, readable carousel lessons for ages 10–14. Each lesson offers a familiar situation, words a learner can use, and one action to practice. Learners can question the examples and suggest better wording.

The aim is practical independence: setting boundaries, respecting others' choices, choosing what to share, and making decisions about attention. Independence includes knowing when to ask for support.

## Common teaching approach
Use plain language and fictional group-chat, gaming, or everyday situations. Demonstrate one response, invite the learner to try their own, then let them explain it to someone else: see one, do one, teach one. Participation and personal disclosure are optional.

Short phrases should help recall. They are prompts for judgment, rather than instructions to repeat a slogan regardless of context. Modeling and imitation describe the mimetic aspect. Memorable phrases that can circulate describe the memetic aspect. Neither label establishes a new technology or proven learning effect.

## Boundaries and resilience
A learner can set a boundary the first time. “Stay calm, set a firm boundary, then redirect” can accompany “Stop making it personal. We can talk about something else.”

There is no requirement to remain calm perfectly, endure repeated mistreatment, or demonstrate toughness before seeking help. Threats, fear, and persistent bullying are reasons to get support immediately. Reconciliation does not require restoring access or accepting further mistreatment.

## Practical privacy
Teach purpose, audience, and proportionate access: does the recipient need this information, who can see it, and can access be removed? Use fictional examples, never real passwords, home addresses, or private messages as lesson submissions.

An earlier yes does not mean permission forever. A learner can ask someone to stop sharing a photo, stop location sharing, leave a chat, or review app permissions. They should respect another person's refusal too. These examples do not claim that someone can unilaterally cancel every legal obligation or erase every existing copy.

Where an age-appropriate app supports it, a 24-hour disappearing-message timer can reduce retained chat history. Availability and timer behavior vary by service. A timer cannot prevent screenshots, recordings, or copying, and should not be presented as making a conversation safe. It does not automatically erase live party-chat recordings. Preserve ordinary evidence of bullying or threats when safe, report it, and seek help even if evidence is missing.

Primary references:
- [WhatsApp: disappearing messages](https://faq.whatsapp.com/673193694148537/)
- [Signal: set and manage disappearing messages](https://support.signal.org/hc/en-us/articles/360007320771-Set-and-manage-disappearing-messages)

Teach that information could be shared without claiming that it has been shared or that someone is monitoring the learner. Finish a reasonable check and return to the activity. Responsibility for misuse belongs to the person misusing the information.

## Attention and learning
[Lesson 04](../lessons/04-attention-and-your-feed.md) invites noticing, choosing, and switching without guilt. Entertainment and rest are valid choices. A learner can turn curiosity into a small project without having to make every minute productive.

## Feedback and evaluation
Comments, likes, saves, and shares indicate engagement, not proof of comprehension or improved wellbeing. Optional public prompts can ask which line was unclear or which fictional example would be useful.

For learning feedback, invite a learner to explain a choice in a new fictional scenario. Check whether they can adapt the idea, respect someone else's boundary, recognize limits, and identify help. A qualified educator's review could identify confusing or unsuitable wording. No evaluation results are reported.

Do not solicit identifying information, screenshots of private conversations, or sensitive disclosures from children. There is no requirement to comment or share to benefit from a lesson.

## Carousel publishing standard
- Primary format: ten-slide carousel, with an editable source deck.
- Ages: 10–14, with readable text, concrete examples, and limited copy per slide.
- Theme rotation by lesson: spring, summer, autumn, winter, then repeat.
- Bottom center: “ITI. Keep building”.
- Bottom right: month and year, page number, and “Swipe to the right” on each slide.
- Editorial plan after the master-deck promotion: 8 a.m. Eastern for catalog-topic content and 6 p.m. Eastern for Everyday Lessons.
- The times are editorial choices, not proven audience peaks. This release does not schedule or confirm social posts. Access should respect each platform's age rules.

## Limits and related work
This is educational content under development, not a school accreditation, tested curriculum, therapeutic intervention, or claim to reverse harmful habits. Good intentions and AI assistance do not establish accuracy or effectiveness. The purpose is explicit and learners retain room to disagree.
