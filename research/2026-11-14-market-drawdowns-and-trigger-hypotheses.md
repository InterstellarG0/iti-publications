# Market Drawdowns and Trigger Hypotheses

Date: 2026-11-14
Status: Published research question v0.1; no completed cross-asset study.
Provenance: Founder challenged whether a prolonged, broad market correction without one major external catastrophe is historically unusual. ChatGPT/Orion organized the question and preserved the objection to an overly narrow weekly comparison.

## The question and correction
Has the selected period exhibited unusually deep, simultaneous drawdowns across specified assets relative to comparable historical periods? Does that pattern require a single dramatic external shock?

A daily decline, a weekly gain, and a continuing peak-to-current drawdown can all coexist. A weekly rebound cannot by itself refute a claim about a longer decline. Likewise, a large decline in one asset cannot establish synchronized declines across all asset classes.

## Define the comparison before answering
Specify the assets, currency, starting and ending dates, price or total-return treatment, and data sources. Measure both returns over common dates and drawdowns from each series' own peak; these answer different questions. A shared decline is not itself a calculated correlation, evidence of hidden coordination, or proof of one common cause.

Distinguish information known at the time from explanations assigned afterward. Market-internal mechanisms, financing conditions, changing expectations, and external events may overlap. The absence of a catastrophe on the scale of war or a major attack does not mean an absence of economically relevant news.

## Historical evidence boundaries
Federal Reserve research on 1987 describes how investor responses and market mechanisms contributed to a severe crash. Its December 2018 minutes discuss falling equity prices, widening credit spreads, growth concerns, and trade tensions. These are counterexamples to requiring one catastrophic external event; they do not prove equivalence to the present cross-asset pattern.

The 2018 minutes also describe falling Treasury yields, illustrating why an equity decline should not automatically be labeled an all-assets decline: yields and existing bond prices generally move in opposite directions.
